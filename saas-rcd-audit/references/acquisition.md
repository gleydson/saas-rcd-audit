# Acquisition — ICP, landing page, copy, positioning

> Acquisition is where churn and CAC are *born*. A vague ICP and a feature-first landing page leak money before the funnel even starts. Building the product is the easy part; **distribution is the game.**

## ICP gate (finding #1 if it fails)
A clear ICP is not "everyone who wants the product." It's a deliberately chosen **underserved niche** that:
1. **Feels the pain with real weight** — the loss when unresolved is large. A CRM is worth more to a facial-aesthetics clinic (a lost lead = R$3,000) than to a barber (R$60). Pain is proportional to what's lost.
2. **Is big enough** — bottom-up TAM closes the math. The common traction-stage error is the *opposite* of niche-too-small: hugging the whole world out of TAM fear and losing the specificity that sells.
3. **Can pay** — the ICP absorbs the ticket your unit economics require.
4. **Has founder fit** — domain you know gives native language, a fast validation network, and instinct about what the customer will accept.

Why it matters for the audit: **with a clear ICP, errors have a diagnosis** ("wrong message, the real pain is X"). Without it, the probability box explodes — you can't tell if you missed on product, copy, channel, price, or audience, and every test is expensive and teaches little. In traction, erring is normal; erring *without knowing why* burns the runway. *ERP genérico* is hard to sell; *ERP para marcenaria* is another conversation.

## The 5-second hero test
Show the top of the landing page to someone for 5 seconds, hide it, and ask: **what does this product do? for whom?** If they can't answer, the hero failed — no matter how beautiful it is. The visitor's brain runs three questions, in order:
1. **What is this?**
2. **Is this for me?**
3. **Why should I care now?**

Most BR SaaS heroes answer zero of three because they traded clarity for "creativity." "Revolucione seu fluxo de trabalho" doesn't say what it does; "A plataforma definitiva de produtividade" doesn't say for whom. The principle is **cognitive load**: every second spent decoding you is a second closer to leaving. Clarity beats cleverness at the top of the funnel.

## Hero anatomy
- **Headline:** what the product does, in the customer's language (not internal jargon).
- **Subheadline:** nails the ICP + the concrete result.
- **Real visual proof:** a screenshot of the product working — not a generic illustration of smiling people.
- **Specific CTA:** see CTA section.
- **Immediate social proof:** a logo or number, above the fold.

## Problem-first & specificity
People don't wake up wanting "a customizable report feature." They wake up to a boss asking for the churn number, worried it'll take 2 hours. **Dor first; feature as the solution.**
- "Plataforma de gestão de projetos com IA" → meh. "Seu time perde 6 horas por semana procurando informação" → keep reading.
- Specificity sells: "Software de gestão" is generic; **"Controle de comandas pra oficina mecânica"** says exactly what and for whom, and the niche feels it was built for them.
- A LP that *describes* loses to one that shows the *transformation* the product causes (0.5% vs 3% conversion on the same traffic — the difference is the sequence of arguments and the specificity, not the visual design).

## 5 stages of awareness (Schwartz)
Conversion is a mirror of awareness. Match the message to the level the visitor arrives at:
| Stage | The visitor… | Lead with |
|---|---|---|
| **Unaware** | doesn't know they have the problem | education; name the problem; benchmark/checklist |
| **Problem-aware** | feels the pain, no solution in mind | agitate the pain, name it precisely |
| **Solution-aware** | knows solution types, comparing | honest comparison, category framing |
| **Product-aware** | knows your product, weighing it | differentiation, offer, "why us" |
| **Most-aware** | ready, needs a nudge | offer, reduced friction, fast onboarding |
**Red flag:** a LP answering questions nobody at the incoming traffic's awareness level is asking (e.g. deep feature copy for unaware traffic).

## CTA microcopy & click triggers
The button is the last thing the user reads before deciding — treat it as such. Microcopy beats button color for conversion. A good CTA answers the 3 pre-click questions (**outcome bias / construal level**): *what happens when I click · how long · what does it cost/commit.* Add a **click-trigger** line beneath it to answer the rest and break an objection.
| Context | CTA | Click-trigger |
|---|---|---|
| B2B trial | "Começar trial grátis" | "14 dias, sem cartão" |
| Fast onboarding | "Criar conta em 30 segundos" | "Eleita melhor ferramenta do setor" |
| Pricing | "Assinar e começar a usar hoje" | "25% no plano anual" |
| Checkout | "Pagar R$199 e ativar" | "Entrega grátis em 3 dias" |
"Cadastrar" leaves all three open. "Começar grátis em 30s" answers two.

## The 3 pre-copy variables
The brain processes three things *before* the words; they usually move more than any headline rewrite.
1. **Contrast** — a function of surroundings, not isolated color. A black button on white contrasts more than red-on-orange. The a11y minimum is 4.5:1, but perceptual contrast also involves size, shape, position, and whitespace. A big button with a distinct shape in a breathing zone is *seen before it's read* — and being seen is a precondition for being clicked.
2. **Layout** — Fitts's Law (bigger, closer targets get clicked more — gentle on mouse, aggressive on touch) over the F-pattern (Nielsen Norman eye-tracking: eyes sweep top, a shorter second horizontal sweep, then down the left). Anything outside the F is captured only by strong contrast. A primary CTA in the bottom-right with no visual anchor is off the attention map regardless of copy.
3. **Trust** — familiarity reads as safety. Built in four reinforcing layers: a **real human face** (not stock), a **known brand or specific verifiable proof**, an **offer that matches the user's declared problem**, and a **solution mechanism logically coherent with the promise.** When all four align, the user converts without knowing why; when one fails, they invent a rational reason to leave.

## First screen = first impression
A login/signup screen does a landing page's job without being one. 46% judge credibility by visual design (Stanford), and the weight rises with money/PII. In B2B, **looking secure beats looking big.** Password rules aren't only real security — they're a *signal* of security; the inviter's context and a company-count proof all say "trust me" before the user types a letter. The first screen sets the tone for the whole journey.

## CAC is a filter (the LP qualifies)
High CAC is rarely a media problem — it's usually a *filter* problem, and the LP filters before the form. Three levers that lower CAC without the lead noticing:
- **Copy specificity:** "Fluxo de caixa para prestador de serviço com faturamento R$500k–R$3M" filters better than "Organize seu financeiro."
- **Visible price:** filters out those without budget; sales calls then carry only other objections.
- **A qualification question (or copy that does its job):** an early "Qual seu MRR atual?" — or examples that imply it.
Resistance ("we'll lose leads") is the mistake: you lose the conversations that would have failed anyway. **Measure CAC by closed deal, not by lead** — going from 50 leads/close to 30 leads/close nearly doubles media efficiency with the same spend.
Counterpoint (see effort justification in `cognitive-biases.md`): an 8-field form converts ~12% vs ~34% for email+password — qualify by *behavior* inside the product when you can; add form friction only where it genuinely qualifies.

## Cargo cult: don't clone landing pages
A converting page is the visible shell of an invisible system: customer vocabulary from real research, objection order tuned to the ICP's specific fear, hand-picked social proof, a central promise matched to a stated desire. Cloning copies the cheap part (layout) and misses the expensive part — 4% vs 0.6% on the same offer. **Copy the research process, not the pixels.** Ask: what here is a function of the product vs a function of the customer research behind each line?

## Narrative & Build-in-Public
Stories stick; data slips. A story creates familiarity → trust → perceived value (**narrative + mere-exposure**). Framework (Scartozzoni): **Character → Desire → Obstacle → Transformation** — the customer is the hero, not you. Build-in-Public turns your real day-to-day into the hero's journey and builds the hardest-to-copy moat: identification. **Ethics:** storytelling amplifies truth but can't sustain a lie (Diletto's fake grandfather → CONAR; Do Bem's true story → Coca-Cola acquisition).

## PLG preconditions
PLG is a *consequence* of four structural conditions, not a pitch-deck choice. Most BR B2B SaaS have none and pitch PLG anyway (because it seems to remove the part founders don't master — selling):
1. **TTV < 10 minutes** (no consultant demo / API hand-holding / paid implementation to reach value).
2. **Ticket low enough** to self-serve (roughly < R$1,000; above that a buying committee appears).
3. **Native virality or collaboration** (one user drags in others — Notion/Figma/Slack). A CRM with AI doesn't virally spread; it needs SDR + demo + follow-up.
4. **Huge bottom-up TAM** (PLG needs volume; pitch-deck TAM is fantasy).
**Red flag:** "self-service / PLG" claimed with high-touch sales reality — that's sales-led with a PLG name.

## The first 10 users
The first ~10 users define the product's whole curve. Charge from the start — payment is the cheapest test of real pain (curiosity is free; an open wallet requires a concrete problem). Source them from closed communities, personal reach, or pure guerrilla. RepareCar visited each of its first 25 workshops, collected dense feedback, and only started visual design after **20 paying** workshops; when an owner couldn't pay, the question was "what does the system need to do for you to pay now?" For B2C apps the method shifts (pre-sale with the solution in progress), but the principle holds: **the payment friction is part of the test.**

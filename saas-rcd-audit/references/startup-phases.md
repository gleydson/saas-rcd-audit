# The 4 startup phases (the contextual lens)

> Startups in different phases need different things. ~90% of founders apply advice from the wrong phase — reading about growth loops of a R$10M-ARR company and trying to implement it at R$15k MRR. The wrong map kills more startups than bad execution. Calibrate the audit to the phase: it sets what to prioritize and which design work pays off.

ARR bands are guideposts, not hard lines — place the product by *behavior* (is it proving payment? proving system? proving scale?) as much as by revenue.

---

## Survival — R$0 to R$240k ARR
**Goal: prove someone pays.** You're validating two things at once: (1) the problem exists and hurts enough that someone pays, (2) your solution resolves it in a way that justifies the price. Everything that doesn't serve those two validations is distraction — technical founders err here by **over-building** (12 features when they needed 2). Design serves three jobs:
- **Visual differentiation:** your competitor is a Lovable prototype; looking like a serious company closes deals in commoditized markets.
- **Trust layer:** new product from an unknown company — a polished interface reduces perceived risk ("if they cared about this, they cared about the rest"). (Stanford: 46% judge credibility by visual design.)
- **Speed to value:** the faster the user understands what the product does, the faster they decide to pay; added friction kills conversion.
*Priority checklist sections:* ICP gate, Acquisition (hero/positioning/trust), the value-before-the-ask part of Onboarding.

## Traction — R$240k to R$1.2M ARR
**Goal: was it luck or a system?** Three simultaneous validations: (1) unit economics close (CAC, LTV, payback make sense), (2) channels work (you can acquire predictably), (3) retention holds (who enters stays). If churn isn't mastered here you don't advance — each new customer just plugs the hole of one who left; a leaky bucket can't be filled. **Design here is mostly behavioral:**
- Onboarding that delivers value before asking effort (endowed progress: a user at 30% finishes more than one at 0%).
- Reduce TTV — the faster the user hits the real aha moment, the higher conversion and retention.
- Most SaaS measure feature adoption; they should measure **value realization**.
- Upgrade path using the right levers — loss aversion beats discounting (show what's lost on downgrade > what's gained on upgrade).
- The gap between 5% and 3% monthly churn can mean ~40% more retained revenue over 12 months at the same acquisition effort.
*Priority checklist sections:* Activation/Onboarding, Retention/Churn, Pricing/Expansion, Metrics.

## Product-Market Fit — R$1.2M to R$6M ARR
**Goal: prove scale.** The clearest PMF signal: users arrive on their own, stay, and refer — you stop pushing and start being pulled. Predictability of results shows up (you know that X into the top of funnel yields Y revenue — exactly what an investor wants to see, not isolated growth rate). **Design focuses on retention efficiency:** each churn point you reduce here has disproportionate impact (bigger base, same percentage, larger absolute number). Retention becomes a **margin lever** — less acquisition pressure, more capital to reinvest in product. Heavily behavioral: endowment over features the user customized, healthy sunk cost in accumulated history data.
*Priority checklist sections:* Retention/Churn, Metrics (cohort LTV), Product/Design (Swiss Knife, dashboards), Pricing (expansion).

## Scale — R$6M+ ARR
**Goal: compete for attention.** Competition changes nature — you compete for attention, not just category (Netflix counts Steam as a competitor; both fight for the user's hours). Ask who else competes for your ICP's budget and attention, even in a different category. Machine efficiency becomes an obsession — each 0.1% in conversion or retention is millions in revenue. **Design is continuous optimization with behavioral tests** (small tweaks, large impact at scale) plus **defensibility** (network effects, proprietary data, brand, internationalization).
*Priority checklist sections:* Metrics/Experimentation, Retention (moat/network effects), Product/Design (optimization).

---

## Using the lens
Most technical founders are between **Survival and Traction** — strong at building product, code, and infra, but stuck on converting trials, retaining users, pricing, and designing a journey that delivers value before asking effort. Those are behavioral-design problems that code alone doesn't solve. In the audit, lead with the priority sections for the product's phase, and explicitly flag any recommendation that belongs to a later phase as "not yet."
